One of the more uncomfortable ideas in Alain de Botton's *Status Anxiety* is that meritocracy has a psychological cost.

The appealing version of meritocracy is obvious: people should not be trapped by the circumstances of their birth. Talent and effort should matter. A person should be able to rise.

But de Botton is interested in the other side of that idea.

If the people at the top got there because they deserved it, what does that imply about everyone else?

In an aristocratic society, being poor could at least be explained by the structure of the world. You were born into the wrong family. The hierarchy was unfair and explicit. In a supposedly meritocratic society, failure becomes much more personal. If everyone had a chance and you still did not succeed, the conclusion is increasingly that the problem must be you.

That was the part of *Status Anxiety* I found most interesting.

The book is organized around five causes of status anxiety — lovelessness, snobbery, expectation, meritocracy, and dependence — and five possible responses: philosophy, art, politics, religion, and bohemianism.

Some of those categories are more convincing than others, but the examples around comparison and expectation are sharp.

De Botton points out that we do not usually compare ourselves with everyone in the world. We compare ourselves with the people we consider our peers.

Someone living an objectively comfortable life can feel unsuccessful because a friend from university has a better title, a larger house, or more public recognition. Meanwhile, the fact that both of them live more comfortably than almost anyone a few centuries ago offers basically no emotional protection.

That sounds obvious when written down, but it explains a lot.

The people closest to us are often the people most capable of making us feel behind.

The book also reframes status as something closer to a search for love than a search for money.

Money matters, but part of what people are buying with it is evidence that they matter. The promotion changes how people react to you. The prestigious job gets introduced differently at dinner. The expensive house or impressive title sends a message before you have said anything.

De Botton describes self-esteem as unusually dependent on these small social signals. A person can feel perfectly competent in the morning and then meet someone doing better than them and suddenly reevaluate their entire life.

I found the section on snobbery useful for the same reason.

A snob, in his definition, is not simply someone who likes expensive things. It is someone who takes one part of your identity — your job, family, education, income — and uses it to decide your entire value.

That is a particularly nasty form of status judgment because it encourages everyone involved to do the same thing back.

You stop asking whether someone is interesting or kind or funny and start asking where they work.

The book was published in 2004, before LinkedIn became what it is now and before social media turned peer comparison into something we can do continuously from bed, which makes parts of it feel more relevant rather than less.

A lot of modern professional life is basically an infrastructure for knowing exactly where everyone you once considered comparable to you has ended up.

The solutions section is where I found the book less convincing.

De Botton moves through philosophy, art, politics, religion, and bohemia as alternative ways of deciding what deserves status. The basic idea makes sense: society's ranking system is not inevitable, so we can expose ourselves to other systems of value.

The Stoics can remind you that other people's opinions are not entirely under your control. Art can make ordinary lives seem worthy of attention. Christianity historically gave dignity to people without worldly status. Political movements can challenge the values a society has decided to reward. Bohemian communities can deliberately invert mainstream ideas of success.

I understand the argument.

I'm less sure any of those things actually switch off comparison.

You can reject one hierarchy and very quickly construct another one. The person who no longer cares about corporate status can become intensely concerned about artistic credibility. Someone who thinks conspicuous wealth is vulgar can become extremely proud of having better taste than people with conspicuous wealth.

Even refusing to care about status can become a status performance.

That complication is more interesting to me than the idea that we can escape status anxiety entirely.

I don't think the useful takeaway from *Status Anxiety* is that ambition is bad or that we should stop caring what anyone thinks.

It is that we should be suspicious of the moment when achievement stops being something we want and starts becoming evidence of whether we are worthwhile.

Meritocracy is probably better than aristocracy.

But "people should have the opportunity to succeed" is very different from "successful people deserve more respect."

We collapse those two ideas surprisingly often.
