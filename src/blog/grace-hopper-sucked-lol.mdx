Microsoft sponsored me to go to the Grace Hopper Celebration in Phoenix this week, and I was really excited about it.

Part of that was just getting to go somewhere different for a few days.

Part of it was Grace Hopper itself. I work in WDG under storage and filesystems, where I am very used to being one of relatively few women in the room. So the idea of going to a giant conference full of women in computing sounded amazing.

And part of it was that some of my friends from Waterloo who had ended up in California were going too. I am in Seattle now, so getting to see people I used to see all the time was honestly one of the things I was most looking forward to.

The first impression was kind of surreal.

There were women everywhere.

This sounds dumb to write down, but I am used to looking around a technical room and seeing mostly short hair and hoodies. At Grace Hopper it was like being a drop in an ocean of long hair and ponytails.

I don't think I had ever been in a technical space that looked like that before.

It was cool.

Then I started going to the sessions.

## I thought the technical talks would be more technical

This was probably my biggest surprise.

A few of us went to a GitHub workshop because I assumed it was going to be about something deeper. Given that I literally work on storage and filesystems, I had convinced myself it might get into Git internals, object storage, or something closer to how the system actually worked.

It was basically an introduction to using GitHub and contributing to open source.

To be fair, that was actually how GitHub [described the workshop](https://github.blog/news-insights/github-at-grace-hopper-2014/). We had just projected something much more advanced onto it.

We walked out.

The Chrome session was much more like what I had expected. It was about building Chromium and actually felt like a technical conference session.

That contrast kept happening.

There were definitely technical talks, but a lot of the conference was about careers, confidence, workplace dynamics, identity, and being a woman in tech.

At one point we did a privilege walk.

I actually think there is value in that kind of exercise. It makes differences in people's starting points very visible.

But I also remember sitting there thinking: I came to a computing conference.

Maybe that is unfair. Grace Hopper is obviously about women in computing, not just computing.

Still, I expected more of the second word.

## The male allies panel was ridiculous

One of the main sessions was a panel about male allies.

The panelists were Alan Eustace from Google, Mike Schroepfer from Facebook, Blake Irving from GoDaddy, and Tayloe Stansbury from Intuit.

So there we were, at this enormous conference for women in computing, listening to four men talk about women in computing.

I understand why men need to be involved. Most of the people running tech companies are men. If anything is going to change, obviously they have to care.

But why was the panel entirely men?

Apparently enough people had the same concern that someone had made and handed out **male ally bingo cards** before the session.

The cards had predictable things the panelists might say.

Then Alan Eustace started talking about tech culture and someone in the audience literally stood up and yelled:

**"BINGO!"**

This was a real thing that happened.

There is a [transcript of the panel](https://juliepagano.com/blog/2014/12/02/male-allies-panel-transcript), and reading it later does not make it sound less weird.

A lot of the advice kept coming back to women needing to excel, speak up, be confident, push through barriers, and make themselves heard.

Blake Irving from GoDaddy said it was up to women to be excellent. He also talked about women needing to speak up because sometimes men do not want to hear them.

I remember thinking that this was a strange thing to tell a room full of women who had already made it into computing.

We know we have to be good at our jobs.

That part is not exactly new information.

At the same time, I did not totally identify with the protest response either.

People were angry. There were boos. There were bingo cards. There was a lot of open hostility toward some of what was being said.

And at the time I remember thinking: is this really the best way to get anywhere?

I did not think women just protesting men was going to fix the problem.

I also had absolutely no idea what the better approach was.

I still don't think I had enough experience then to understand what changing an industry actually looks like.

I just knew the panel felt bad, and the reaction to the panel also left me kind of uncomfortable.

## Then Satya said the thing about promotions

Satya Nadella was one of the keynote speakers, which obviously mattered a little more to me because I work at Microsoft.

Maria Klawe asked him what advice he would give women who were uncomfortable asking for a raise.

He said women should have faith that the system would give them the right raises as they went along.

He said it was "good karma."

The room did not like this.

There were boos.

Maria Klawe disagreed with him on stage and said women should know what they are worth and practice asking for it. Satya [apologized afterward](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/microsoft-ceo-says-women-dont-need-to-ask-for-a-raise-but-trust-the-system/).

The weird thing is that I understood what he meant more than most people in that room probably did.

Microsoft actually has a pretty structured promotion process.

There are calibration conversations. Your work is discussed by people other than just your direct manager. Promotions are not literally determined by who is best at walking into an office and demanding one.

So when he said to trust the system, part of me thought: okay, I get where that is coming from.

But then there was a listening circle afterward where women talked about their experiences across the industry.

A lot of them basically said the system was *not* working.

That was harder to dismiss.

Maybe Microsoft's process is better in some ways.

Maybe the idea that the system eventually recognizes good work is true for some people.

But if a huge number of women are telling you that their actual experience is that they get overlooked, underpaid, or ignored unless they advocate aggressively for themselves, then "trust the system" is obviously not a satisfying answer.

I think that was the moment the conference became most confusing for me.

I partly agreed with him.

I also understood why everyone was furious.

## I left feeling kind of betrayed

I don't know if "betrayed" is too dramatic, but that is honestly close to how it felt.

I had been really excited to go.

Microsoft paid for me to be there. I got to see old friends. I got to be surrounded by thousands of women who had chosen the same field I had.

That should have felt incredibly affirming.

Instead I kept wondering why a conference for women in computing seemed to expect so little technical depth from us.

Why were some sessions so junior?

Why was a major session about male allies entirely men?

Why did so much of the advice for fixing the industry still boil down to women needing to be better, louder, more confident, or more persistent?

And why did I also feel uneasy with some of the ways women in the audience were reacting?

I don't have a clean answer.

Maybe that is what bothered me most.

I went in expecting to feel like I had found this huge community of women like me.

Instead I mostly came away feeling like I did not quite fit there either.

Which is a pretty weird feeling to get from a conference that was supposed to make me feel less alone.
