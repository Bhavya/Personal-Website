Banks should block an outside AI agent if they cannot establish what it is allowed to do, but they also need to offer customers a better way to delegate financial tasks. I don’t think passing for the customer should be the price of getting useful work done.

The posts circulating this week make it sound as though rogue agents have started breaking into everyone’s bank accounts. The reporting is more qualified. On October 22, [**Reuters reported that major banks were blocking third-party agents**](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/us-banks-detect-unauthorized-ai-agents-customer-accounts-axios-reports-2026-10-22/), including cases involving customers sharing their credentials. It also reported that Morgan Stanley had blocked outside agents but had not observed instances of rogue or unauthorised agents.

That distinction matters. A customer asking an assistant to help with their money is different from someone breaking into their account, but neither the customer’s password nor a general request for help tells the bank exactly which actions should be permitted.

***The customer can want help without authorising everything***

There is a fairly big gap between asking an agent to find a better savings rate and asking it to move money. There is another gap between approving one transfer and giving it ongoing authority to decide when and where to transfer funds.

If the agent enters through the same session as the customer, those distinctions can become difficult to enforce. The interface may let it see balances, add a recipient, change settings and initiate payments because those are all things the customer can do. The customer may have intended to delegate only one of them.

I would be uncomfortable with a bank accepting a consequential action simply because the agent arrived with valid credentials. I would also be uncomfortable with an agent provider treating a broad instruction as permission to use every capability the login exposes.

The bank needs to recognise the agent as an agent and know which customer it represents, but it also needs a usable record of the authority that customer actually granted. That authority should expire or be revoked without requiring the customer to change their banking password.

***Even a sensible instruction needs the account’s full context***

Imagine asking an assistant to move spare cash into an existing savings account while keeping at least $5,000 in your current account. This is a hypothetical example, but it is exactly the kind of boring financial housekeeping I would want an assistant to handle.

The posted balance is $8,000, so moving $3,000 looks reasonable. Rent of $2,500 is scheduled for tomorrow, though, and moving the full amount would leave the account well below the buffer once rent goes out.

Even if the assistant accounts for rent, there is only $500 available under that instruction. If two authorised workflows each decide to move $300 before either transfer posts, they have collectively exceeded it.

Neither agent needs to be malicious or particularly stupid for this to happen. Each can be following a reasonable interpretation of the instruction while looking at an incomplete picture.

The bank should evaluate the proposed transfer against scheduled payments, pending transfers and commitments already made through other channels. It also needs to prevent two requests from claiming the same remaining allowance. Asking the model to remember the buffer is useful, but the system executing the transfer should enforce it.

The customer’s instruction also doesn’t replace the bank’s own rules. A transfer still needs to meet the applicable account restrictions, regulatory requirements and company policies, even when it is requested by an agent the customer trusts.

***There is already work on how agents should identify themselves***

On October 6, Meta and Sierra [**announced Personal Agent Protocol**](https://sierra.ai/blog/introducing-personal-agent-protocol). Its [**current draft documentation**](https://personalagentprotocol.org/docs/introduction) describes a way for agents to identify themselves and obtain access limited by what the customer and company agree to, with access that can be revoked.

That seems like a useful direction. Customers should be able to choose an assistant and companies should be able to set boundaries around what it can do.

The bank still has to connect that permission to its actual financial systems. A permission to transfer money cannot, by itself, establish how much is safe to move at this moment or whether another request has already committed it.

Approval also needs to refer to a specific action. If I approve $500 going to a particular account, the agent should not quietly substitute a different destination or amount and treat my earlier approval as sufficient. If it is unsure whether the transfer completed, it needs to check the underlying transaction before trying again.

Those are things I would expect the institution to enforce regardless of which model or agent provider made the request.

***Blocking buys time, but customers still need an approved route***

I understand why banks are restricting access. I would rather have an assistant fail to complete a task than have it improvise with my money, and I don’t think customers wanting a feature obliges a bank to enable it before the controls exist.

I do think banks should be using this period to build a narrow, explicit way to delegate actions. A transfer between existing accounts, with a customer-defined limit and confirmation of the outcome, seems like a more useful place to start than debating whether agents should have access in general.

Otherwise, the choice remains frustrating: the assistant can explain what I should do, or I can hand it credentials that expose far more authority than the task requires. I would want my bank to offer something between those options, and I would want it working on that now while the scope is still small enough to get right.
