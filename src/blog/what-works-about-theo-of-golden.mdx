Allen Levi’s *Theo of Golden* has a simple and rather lovely premise. An elderly stranger arrives in a small Georgia town and starts buying the pencil portraits hanging in a coffee shop. He seeks out each person, gives them their portrait and asks about their life. The book follows the conversations and friendships that grow out of this unusual habit.

The portraits give Theo a reason to approach people, but they are also a form of recognition. Someone has taken the time to draw them. Theo thinks each person should have the drawing, and he is willing to spend time hearing their story. The encounters gradually reveal grief, loneliness, regret and relationships that have become difficult. His generosity eventually extends to money, but his willingness to listen is what makes the premise work.

The structure is repetitive, and at nearly 400 pages the pattern becomes noticeable. Levi is openly affectionate toward his characters. Some of the encounters feel arranged to make a point about kindness, and the emotional direction is often easy to anticipate. The novel is sentimental and makes no attempt to hide it.

What I appreciate is the specificity of Theo’s gesture. He doesn’t just tell people they matter. He arrives with something made for them and takes an interest in the person behind the picture. The novel occasionally presses its point too hard, but the idea at its centre is worth spending time with.
