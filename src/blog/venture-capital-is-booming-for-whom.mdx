U.S. venture investment reached [**$515.8 billion through September**](https://nvca.org/pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor/), already above any previous full year. That's a lot of money. It is also a slightly frustrating number to read if you're a founder trying to work out whether fundraising has actually gotten easier.

AI companies received 82.7% of the deal value. The remaining 17.3% was split across everything else. Even that distinction is too broad, because an enormous round for a model company and a first check into a small AI startup both end up in the same category.

The conversation around venture tends to swing between “funding is back” and “funding is dead.” I don't find either particularly useful. A record tells me how much money moved. It doesn't tell me how many founders had a realistic chance of receiving any of it.

***The biggest rounds tell us surprisingly little about everyone else***

The biggest AI companies can raise sums that used to be associated with public markets. Their capital needs are real: computing infrastructure, research, talent, and distribution are expensive. A large round in that category can move the total for an entire quarter.

That doesn't tell us much about a seed-stage healthcare startup, a hardware company, or an enterprise software team without an obvious AI label. Even within AI, money flowing to a handful of model and infrastructure companies doesn't mean every application startup can raise easily.

If I'm trying to raise a seed round, I care about whether investors who understand my category are writing checks at that stage. I care about what evidence they expect and whether the company I want to build fits the outcomes they need. The industry can deploy another hundred billion dollars without changing those answers very much.

***The investor side is concentrated too***

The same [**PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor**](https://nvca.org/pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor/) reports that funds of $500 million or more received 78.1% of U.S. VC fundraising dollars through Q3, despite making up just 6% of funds closed. First-time funds received about 4.5%.

That matters because a smaller fund can have a different set of interests from a very large one. A check or an eventual exit that would be meaningful to one fund might barely register for another. A newer manager may also have a different network or a reason to spend time in a category that established firms have overlooked.

None of that makes a first-time manager automatically better. It does mean that concentrating fundraising in large, established firms can change the range of companies venture is set up to support. It also doesn't mean a useful business has no financing options. Revenue, angels, smaller funds, and other sources of capital still exist. But “there is more venture money than ever” is a poor substitute for understanding which of those options actually fits.

***I would rather know how widely the money is reaching***

I'd rather know how many companies are raising a first round, how many get to a second, and whether that is happening across a wider range of sectors. I'd also want to know whether exits are returning money to investors, because that affects their willingness to commit to the next fund.

The total is still useful. It shows that investors are willing to commit extraordinary amounts to certain opportunities. I just think we should be much more careful about turning that into a statement about what it feels like to raise money right now.
