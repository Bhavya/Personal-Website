U.S. venture investment reached [$515.8 billion through September](https://nvca.org/pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor/), already above any previous full year. If you're a founder trying to raise, that number might sound like excellent news.

Then you look at where the money went. AI companies received 82.7% of the deal value. That means the remaining 17.3% was split across everything else. The headline describes a lot of capital moving, but it doesn't describe a broadly easy fundraising market.

I'm interested in this because the conversation around venture tends to swing between “funding is back” and “funding is dead.” Both are too broad. The market can set a record while many founders have fewer plausible investors than they did a few years ago.

## A few enormous rounds change the headline

The biggest AI companies can raise sums that used to be associated with public markets. Their capital needs are real: computing infrastructure, research, talent, and distribution are expensive. A large round in that category can move the total for an entire quarter.

That doesn't tell us much about a seed-stage healthcare startup, a hardware company, or an enterprise software team without an obvious AI label. Even within AI, money flowing to a handful of model and infrastructure companies doesn't mean every application startup can raise easily.

The important question for a founder is not how many dollars the industry deployed. It's whether investors who understand their category are writing checks at their stage and what evidence those investors now expect.

## The investor side is concentrated too

The same [PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor](https://nvca.org/pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor/) reports that funds of $500 million or more received 78.1% of U.S. VC fundraising dollars through Q3, despite making up just 6% of funds closed. First-time funds received about 4.5%.

That matters downstream. Smaller and newer managers are often willing to spend time on companies that don't fit the current consensus. If fewer of them can raise, founders may have fewer kinds of investors to talk to, even while total venture capital grows.

I'm not claiming every first-time fund would make better bets. I do think a market dominated by established firms and unusually large rounds has different incentives from one with a healthy range of fund sizes. A billion-dollar fund cannot build its returns by writing only small checks into modest outcomes.

## What the record actually says

The figures say investors are willing to put extraordinary amounts of capital behind a relatively narrow set of opportunities. They do not say that the average startup has an easier path to financing.

If I were looking at the health of the venture market, I'd want to track the spread: how many companies raise a first round, how many get to a second, which sectors attract new funds, and whether exits are returning money to investors. The total dollars invested is one measure. Right now, it may be the least representative one.
