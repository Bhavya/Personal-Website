A four-person engineering team can now have several patches in progress without assigning someone to write each one. It’s easy to see how that gets people to ask whether four engineers could do the work that used to take eight. In some teams they might, but that depends on how much of the work was writing the patch in the first place.

The studies we have measure different parts of the job. In [**METR’s early 2025 randomized study**](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), experienced open-source developers took 19% longer to finish tasks in repositories they knew well when AI tools were available, even though they came away feeling faster. METR’s [**February 2026 follow-up**](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) suggested the newer tools were helping more, but who took part and which tasks they put into the experiment made the size of the improvement hard to estimate.

I use AI coding tools constantly and they’ve changed how much work I can start on my own. The time it takes to produce a patch can be quite different from the time it takes to establish that the patch belongs in the system. A retry can pass the tests and still run a payment twice if the first request succeeded before timing out. Someone has to know how the service behaves in that situation, especially if the code and tests were written with the same assumption.

In [**METR’s review of patches that passed SWE-bench Verified**](https://metr.org/notes/2026-03-10-many-swe-bench-passing-prs-would-not-be-merged-into-main/), maintainers said they wouldn’t merge roughly half of the sampled patches from the agents studied. That doesn’t tell us what fraction of agent-written code gets rejected in general, but it shows why passing an automated grader is an incomplete measure of work done. In a [**longitudinal study of professional engineers**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23135), participants also reported spending less time writing code, and the authors describe the work of directing and checking AI output as supervisory engineering work. Some of the time saved implementing a change can move into that work.

***Review capacity and team size***

With several agents working in parallel, an engineer can get more changes ready for review without gaining any more time to understand them. Two patches might each look reasonable on their own and still be hard to release together if they depend on incompatible assumptions about the same API. The manager and technical lead have to account for that when deciding how much work the team takes on. When the same engineer needs to review both changes and will be the one on call for them, starting another patch can make the queue longer without getting either change into production sooner.

A team with reliable tests and clear service boundaries has more room to work this way. It can verify more of a change without asking the person who remembers how every downstream service behaves, and it can deploy a narrower change without coordinating a release with half the organization. In a tightly coupled system with slow tests, a small team can find itself spending most of the time it saved on implementation waiting for feedback or chasing down context. That fits the conclusion of [**Google’s 2025 DORA report**](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/): the systems a team already uses to deliver software shape how much it gets out of AI.

***Ownership and how engineers learn***

An engineer who can explain how a service should behave and recognize when a patch violates that expectation has more room to delegate implementation. Knowing the system well enough to do that takes time, which is one reason I’m wary of making teams even more senior-heavy. I’ve written about [**the problems that come with senior-heavy teams**](/blog/what-senior-heavy-teams-make-harder), and AI makes it easier to give senior engineers all the design and review work while everyone else manages agent output.

A junior or mid-level engineer needs chances to own a change and see how it behaves in production. Debugging a failure yourself can teach you something that reading an agent’s explanation doesn’t, especially when the explanation sounds plausible. If the same senior engineer always takes over when the work gets ambiguous, the rest of the team has fewer opportunities to build the experience that makes it possible to share review and on-call responsibility later.

***Measuring delivery with a smaller team***

A four-person team opening as many pull requests as an eight-person team doesn’t tell me whether it can take on the same scope. I care more about how long a useful change takes to reach production and how much of the team’s time it takes to keep that change working. If the release gets out sooner but a senior engineer spends the next week on regressions, the cost shows up after the pull request closes.

The near-term gain may be that a good team can own more without growing. That seems more plausible to me than assuming that adopting coding agents is enough to justify cutting a team in half. If I’m still relying on the same two engineers to review changes and answer for them in production, I don’t have much evidence that the team can get smaller. I may have a good case for giving those two engineers fewer changes to deal with at once.
