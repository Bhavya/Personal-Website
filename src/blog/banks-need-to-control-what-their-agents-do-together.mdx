---
title: "Banks need to control what their agents do together"
description: "A dispute agent can handle a case correctly while another agent collects the disputed amount. I think banks need to evaluate those actions together."
tags:

date: "2026-10-19"

I think a lot of the work involved in bringing AI agents into a bank will be getting them to respect decisions made elsewhere in the institution. An agent can understand its assignment and still take an action that conflicts with another team's work.

Consider a customer disputing $900 of a $1,200 credit-card balance. A servicing agent opens the investigation. Meanwhile, an agent managing autopay prepares to collect the full balance. Each has a fairly understandable task. The customer still ends up paying money they were entitled to withhold.

That is the kind of situation I would want resolved before giving either agent more autonomy.

***The dispute changes what other workflows are allowed to do***

For this hypothetical, assume the customer has submitted a qualifying written billing-error notice within the required 60-day window, and the card issuer receives it more than three business days before its own scheduled autopay. Assume there are no related charges to account for.

[**Regulation Z's billing-error rules**](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/13/) protect the disputed amount during resolution. Under the specified autopay conditions, the issuer cannot deduct that amount or related finance or other charges. The undisputed $300 can still be paid.

Opening a case is therefore only part of the work. The dispute has changed what the bank can collect. That change has to reach the system authorizing the debit before the debit happens.

Giving both agents a copy of the regulation would not make me comfortable that this will happen. The autopay agent needs the current dispute status, and the service executing the collection needs to enforce it. A conversation in the servicing agent's history is a fairly poor place to keep a fact that other workflows must respect.

***The bank also needs to know what it has already committed to***

Suppose a separate customer-service interaction concerns compensation for the inconvenience. The bank has a hypothetical policy allowing up to $200 in discretionary goodwill credits over 30 days without further approval.

The customer has received $50. Another $80 is approved but has not posted. An agent proposes an additional $100. Looking only at completed credits makes the request appear to fit. Counting the pending commitment brings the total to $230.

These goodwill credits are separate from any correction or protection the law requires. A discretionary budget cannot become a reason to withhold mandatory relief.

I would want the proposed action recorded before execution, with enough detail to establish the customer, amount, purpose, and authority. Checking the total and recording a new commitment have to happen together, so two agents cannot both use the same remaining allowance. If the extra credit needs human approval, the reviewer should see the existing $130 commitment too. Approval should apply to the specific action reviewed, rather than becoming permission for whatever the agent decides to do next.

***I would still check whether the change actually happened***

Return to the autopay. The agent submits an instruction to reduce it to $300, but the payment system rejects the update. The agent has followed the intended process. The original $1,200 debit is still scheduled.

A record that says “update requested” cannot support telling the customer that autopay has been corrected. I would want confirmation from the payment system, with an unresolved result kept open and routed to someone who can intervene before collection.

The same applies to the pending $80 credit. Another agent should not assume it failed because confirmation is late and issue a replacement. It also should not assume the customer has received it. Both decisions depend on establishing what actually happened.

These coordination problems already exist in financial software. Agents make them easier to reproduce across more workflows, especially when different teams buy or build their agents separately. I think banks will get more useful automation when they can check a proposed action against shared obligations and commitments, then verify the outcome. That gives them a way to expand what agents can do without asking each agent to reconstruct the bank's position from its own limited view.
