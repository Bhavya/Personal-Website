Cursor officially became part of SpaceX today.

The deal was announced in June at a reported $60 billion valuation, all in stock. Cursor's explanation for the acquisition is straightforward: SpaceX has enormous compute capacity, and Cursor wants to train stronger models more cheaply.

That is a real advantage.

I am still not convinced the acquisition was the best move for Cursor as a product.

Obviously, I understand why the founders and investors would take the deal. A $60 billion outcome is an extraordinary result for a company that is only a few years old.

But the thing I valued most about Cursor was not that it had its own model.

It was that Cursor sat above the model layer.

## Cursor benefited from not having to pick a winner

One of Cursor's strongest positions was that it could simply use whatever model was best.

OpenAI gets better at one kind of coding task? Use OpenAI.

Anthropic gets better at long-running agents? Use Claude.

Google ships something better? Add Gemini.

Cursor can train its own models where that makes sense and use third-party models everywhere else.

That is a very attractive position in a market where the underlying models keep leapfrogging each other.

The product does not need to predict who wins the model race.

It just needs to make the best models useful.

I think that neutrality was strategically important.

Once Cursor is owned by SpaceX, which now has its own AI ambitions through xAI, that position gets more complicated.

Even if Cursor continues offering competing models, it is no longer an independent layer sitting above them.

There is now a parent company with a very obvious interest in making its own models better and more widely used.

That does not mean Cursor will suddenly become a Grok-only product.

It does mean the incentives have changed.

## The compute argument is compelling, but it cuts both ways

The acquisition announcement talks heavily about compute.

Cursor says SpaceX gives it access to the largest fleet of GPUs in the world and will let it build stronger models at lower cost.

That matters.

Model training is expensive, inference is expensive, and coding agents are increasingly doing long-running work rather than producing a few autocomplete tokens.

Owning or having privileged access to large amounts of compute can materially change the economics.

But I am not sure Cursor needed to become part of a model company to get that benefit.

It could buy compute.

It could sign infrastructure partnerships.

It could raise more money.

It could continue using external models while selectively training its own.

Those options may be more expensive, but they preserve something that is difficult to get back once it is gone: strategic independence.

## I think Cursor's application layer may be more valuable than its model layer

There is a tendency right now to treat model ownership as the highest form of defensibility.

I am not sure that is true for Cursor.

Cursor built an unusually good interface between developers and models.

It understood codebases.

It built the workflows around editing, agents, context, review, and iteration.

It became the place where developers could benefit from model competition without having to care very much about which company trained the model underneath.

That relationship with the developer is incredibly valuable.

The models may become more interchangeable over time.

The workflow does not necessarily become interchangeable.

If that happens, owning the developer surface could be more strategically important than owning another frontier model.

That is why selling Cursor to a company with a model agenda feels strange to me.

The independent version of Cursor could have been the Switzerland of coding models.

The acquired version has a home team.

## There is also a trust question

Developer tools occupy a weirdly sensitive position.

Cursor can potentially see a huge amount of a company's source code, architecture, internal systems, and engineering behavior.

Enterprise customers already care about where that data goes and which models receive it.

Adding a large parent company with its own AI business does not automatically make Cursor unsafe.

But it does add another question to procurement.

Who ultimately controls the product?

What incentives does the parent company have?

Will model selection remain neutral?

Will data boundaries remain exactly the same over time?

Those questions existed before.

I think acquisition makes them more salient.

## The obvious counterargument is that independence was going to get harder anyway

Cursor is competing against some of the best-capitalized companies in the world.

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and others all have strong incentives to own more of the developer workflow themselves.

The coding assistant is becoming too important to remain a peripheral feature.

An independent Cursor would have had to keep paying for models and compute while competing with the companies selling it those inputs.

That is not a comfortable position either.

SpaceX gives Cursor capital, compute, distribution, and the ability to train models without being completely dependent on external providers.

I can see the strategic logic.

I just think the price of that security is higher than the acquisition announcement makes it sound.

## The founders probably made an excellent decision for themselves

I want to separate two things that often get collapsed.

A great acquisition outcome for founders is not necessarily the same thing as the best long-term outcome for the product.

Selling at $60 billion is an astonishing financial result.

If I were sitting across the table from that offer, I am not going to pretend the decision would be easy to reject.

But from the perspective of Cursor itself, I think something important was given up.

Cursor had become valuable partly because it did not need to belong to any particular model company.

It could be the layer that benefited no matter who won underneath.

Now it belongs to one of the competitors.

Maybe the compute advantage ends up mattering more.

Maybe Cursor becomes dramatically better because it can train models at a scale that would have been difficult independently.

Maybe SpaceX leaves the product unusually autonomous.

All of that could happen.

I just think the independent position was more valuable than it looked.

And unlike compute, independence is not something Cursor can easily buy back later.
