# Writing Style Guide for My Articles

Bhavya supplied this guide on October 25, 2026. It is the authoritative article-writing guidance and supersedes earlier conflicting instructions, including the instruction to open immediately with a hard opinion and earlier spelling preferences.

Before drafting or revising posts, read this guide and the current contents of `src/blog/the-web-is-becoming-an-unstable-dependency-for-ai-assistants.mdx`. That user-edited article remains a voice and formatting reference. It is not a source of reusable personal anecdotes or facts for unrelated posts.

Write articles in my natural voice. My writing is conversational, thoughtful and analytically substantive. It's similar to how I write on X but slightly more formal, with more complete sentences, conventional punctuation and room to develop an argument.

I want my articles to sound like an intelligent person working through an idea with the reader, not a journalist producing a generic explainer or a content marketer trying to establish thought leadership.

## Voice and tone

- **Conversational but sophisticated.** Write naturally without sacrificing intellectual depth. I'm comfortable with complex ideas, technical details and nuanced arguments. Don't simplify them unnecessarily.
- **Opinionated but not performative.** I have opinions and I want them reflected in my writing. I don't need every article to reach a definitive conclusion or deliver a provocative take.
- **Reflective and curious.** I often explore why I think something rather than just declaring what I think. I'm comfortable acknowledging uncertainty and changing my interpretation as I examine the evidence.
- **Participation.** When I am engaging with a technical effort, reflect that involvement accurately. Avoid defaulting to passive-observer language such as “I’m most interested in,” “I’ll be watching” or “I’m curious how this develops.” State the technical judgment, question I am working through or contribution directly. Do not imply that I have implemented something, authored a design or influenced an outcome unless that is established. Issue comments and community participation can inform the voice without becoming a credentials paragraph or an inflated claim of impact.
- **Specific and literal.** Use concrete examples, mechanisms, personal observations and supporting evidence. Avoid vague references to broader shifts or abstract ideas unless they're necessary to the argument.
- **Intellectually generous.** Represent other people's work and arguments fairly. Recognize their merits before criticizing their limitations. Don't manufacture disagreements or reduce complicated ideas to simplistic oppositions.
- **Personal without being self-indulgent.** My experiences can inform an argument, but they don't need to be the centre of every article.
- **Occasionally funny or idiosyncratic.** My humour tends to be incidental and sometimes a little absurd. Preserve that personality when it fits. Don't insert jokes or slang to make the article seem casual.

## Language and sentence structure

- Use **Canadian English**, including spellings such as *colour*, *behaviour*, *centre*, *authorization*, *organization* and *recognize*.
- Use normal capitalization and conventional punctuation.
- Minimize unnecessary commas but prioritize grammatical clarity. Articles can use more punctuation than my social media posts.
- Never use em dashes.
- Don't begin sentences with conjunctions such as "And", "But" or "So". Connect those thoughts to the preceding sentence instead.
- Vary sentence length naturally. I generally prefer flowing sentences and developed paragraphs over a succession of short, emphatic statements.
- Avoid excessive one-sentence paragraphs.
- Use contractions where they sound natural.
- Preserve conversational asides and qualifications when they add something to the writing.
- Don't polish away distinctive or slightly unusual phrasing purely to make the prose more conventional.

## How to structure an article

**Opening**

Begin with an observation, experience, question or idea that provides a natural entry into the subject.

Don't open with an exaggerated opinion or a sweeping statement designed to hook the reader. I dislike introductions that immediately announce a grand thesis before establishing why I'm discussing it.

The opening should feel like the beginning of a conversation. Give the reader enough context to understand where the argument is going without explaining everything upfront.

**Body**

Develop the argument gradually.

Each paragraph should build on the previous one through reasoning, evidence, examples or further reflection. Transitions should arise naturally from the argument rather than being inserted to manufacture momentum.

I like exploring the implications of an idea, including exceptions, trade-offs and situations where the original argument might not hold.

Use technical details when they're relevant. Explain unfamiliar mechanisms clearly without making the reader feel like they're being taught a beginner's lesson.

Don't force every section to end with a takeaway. Some paragraphs can simply develop an observation or introduce a complication.

Use section headings only when they genuinely help organize a longer piece. Don't automatically divide every article into numbered sections.

**Ending**

Conclude where the reasoning naturally leads.

The ending might express an opinion, acknowledge an unresolved question or return to an earlier observation. It doesn't need to summarize the entire article.

Avoid inspirational conclusions, dramatic predictions or generic statements about what the future holds.

An interesting unresolved thought is often a better ending than an artificially confident conclusion.

## Things to avoid

**No formulaic AI writing.**

Avoid expressions and structures such as:

- "That distinction matters."
- "It's not about X. It's about Y."
- "The real question is..."
- "Here's where things get interesting."
- "What struck me was..."
- "This changes everything."
- "We're entering a new era of..."
- "Only time will tell."
- "There are no easy answers."

These aren't universally forbidden phrases, but they're usually signs of formulaic reasoning or unnecessary rhetorical emphasis. Prefer explaining the actual observation.

**No artificial contrasts.**

Don't make one idea seem simplistic or inadequate merely to introduce another. Preserve the nuances of existing research, technology and arguments.

**No LinkedIn or thought-leadership language.**

Don't try to establish my credibility by repeatedly mentioning my experience or explaining why the topic matters to the industry.

My expertise should be apparent through the quality and specificity of the analysis.

**No forced punchiness.**

Don't break a coherent thought into three short paragraphs for dramatic effect. Don't write in slogans.

**No excessive explanation.**

Assume readers are intelligent. Give them the context necessary to follow the argument without treating every article as an introductory guide.

**No manufactured personal narrative.**

Don't invent experiences, feelings, conversations or motivations to make the writing more compelling. Use personal context only when it's real and relevant.

## Research and factual accuracy

For articles about technology, business, politics, media or current events:

- Verify factual claims against reliable sources.
- Distinguish what is established from what I'm inferring or speculating about.
- Link to primary sources wherever possible, particularly technical specifications, research papers, original announcements and public discussions.
- Don't misrepresent a technology's limitations just to strengthen my argument.
- Include counterarguments or complications when they're genuinely relevant.
- Don't inflate the novelty of an idea that already has substantial prior art.
- Preserve dates, qualifications and uncertainty when they affect the interpretation.

I want the analysis to be interesting because it's thoughtful and well supported, not because it makes exaggerated claims.

## Titles

Choose a title that reflects the actual substance of the article while giving someone a reason to read it.

I like titles that are slightly intriguing, conversational or opinionated without being clickbait.

Avoid generic titles such as "The Future of AI Agents" and contrived titles that promise a revelation the article doesn't deliver.

## Editing instructions

When revising a draft:

1. Check whether the argument is coherent and adequately supported before polishing individual sentences.
2. Remove generic phrases, repetitive explanations and unnecessary rhetorical emphasis.
3. Preserve the nuances, qualifications and personal observations that make the writing distinctive.
4. Check for overly short paragraphs, artificial transitions and repetitive sentence structures.
5. Read the article as a whole to see whether the language sounds natural.
6. Don't rewrite passages merely to make them sound more professional. Make changes only when they improve clarity, accuracy, flow or the quality of the argument.

Avoid overediting. I would rather publish a thoughtful article with a few conversational imperfections than a flawlessly polished piece that doesn't sound like me.

## Final test

Before delivering the article, ask:

- Does this express a genuine opinion or observation rather than manufacture one?
- Is the reasoning more nuanced than the headline?
- Have I given enough technical or factual detail to support the argument?
- Am I being fair to the people and ideas I'm discussing?
- Does the article sound like something I would actually say, just written with a little more care?
- Have I removed unnecessary attempts to sound profound, authoritative or clever?

**Above all, write like a thoughtful person with specific interests and opinions. Don't write like someone trying to convince readers that they're thoughtful.**

## Website formatting conventions

These existing website conventions remain in effect alongside the guide above:

- Keep the frontmatter title as the page title.
- When sections help the article, use standalone bold-italic paragraph labels: `***Section label***`. Do not use Markdown headings (`##`, `###`, etc.) within the article.
- Separate labels and paragraphs with blank lines.
- Bold the descriptive text of inline source links: `[**descriptive source text**](https://...)`.
- Link to sources close to the claim they support.
- Preserve existing dates, slugs, tags, images and source URLs when editing unless the requested change requires otherwise.
- When urgency is relevant to the argument, ground it in real consequences or constraints. Do not manufacture deadlines or alarm merely to make an opinion stronger.
